## Email 2 - Day 2

**Subject:** A workout can make you tired without making you capable

**Preview text:** The gap traditional fitness often misses.

Hi {{ subscriber.first_name | default: "there" }},

Most fitness plans are very good at measuring work: pounds lifted, calories burned, miles completed, or sweat produced.

Those numbers can matter. But real life asks a different question:

**Can your body organize the right response when you need it?**

You may exercise consistently and still notice that you feel stiffer, slower, less balanced, or less coordinated than you used to. That does not always mean you need a harder workout. It may mean your training is developing some qualities while ignoring others.

Complete capability includes:

- movement you can control
- strength you can use
- balance you can trust
- coordination under changing demands
- power when you need to move quickly
- recovery that keeps you ready for life

This is the idea behind ALLEIN: train the whole capability profile instead of chasing exhaustion for its own sake.

For your next workout, ask yourself one question afterward: **What did this session prepare me to do better?**

Michael

## Email 3 - Day 4

**Subject:** You do not have to compete to be an athlete

**Preview text:** The Everyday Athlete is an identity built around readiness.

Hi {{ subscriber.first_name | default: "there" }},

When people hear the word athlete, they often picture a uniform, a scoreboard, or a younger version of themselves.

I use the phrase **Everyday Athlete** differently.

An Everyday Athlete is someone who wants their body to remain prepared for life. That might mean:

- keeping up with children or grandchildren
- reacting quickly when something unexpected happens
- carrying luggage, groceries, or equipment without hesitation
- hiking, skiing, playing a sport, or returning to an old hobby
- getting to the floor and back up confidently
- feeling athletic again instead of merely avoiding decline

You do not need to perform like a collegiate athlete. I know that world from my years as an NCAA decathlete, and the training volume is not the goal.

The useful lesson is that athletic qualities can be trained intentionally at any appropriate level. Strength, balance, coordination, power, and recovery are not reserved for competitors. They are human capabilities.

What would being more capable allow you to do that matters to you?

Hit reply and tell me. That answer is often more useful than a generic goal like "get in shape."

Michael

## Email 4 - Day 6

**Subject:** How ALLEIN turns a starting point into a plan

**Preview text:** Assess, build, coach, and reassess.

Hi {{ subscriber.first_name | default: "there" }},

A useful program should fit the person doing it.

That sounds obvious, but many people are handed a collection of exercises without enough attention to their goals, history, schedule, equipment, confidence, or recovery.

ALLEIN coaching follows a four-part process:

**1. Assess**  
Establish a starting point across movement, strength, balance, coordination, power, and recovery.

**2. Build**  
Create an individualized plan around your life, equipment, experience, and priorities.

**3. Coach**  
Use technique feedback, accountability, and regular adjustments to keep the plan appropriate and useful.

**4. Reassess**  
Measure what changed, identify the next opportunity, and continue building capability.

The nervous system is part of that process because movement begins with information. Your brain and body continually interpret the environment, prepare for movement, coordinate force, and update future responses.

That does not require gimmicks or a magical "brain hack." It requires purposeful practice, progressive training, useful sensory information, and an appropriate challenge.

That is the standard I want ALLEIN to represent.

Michael
